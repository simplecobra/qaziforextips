Interbank rate vs open market rate

Pakistan has two dollar rates. The interbank rate is the rate banks trade at with each other; it is the rate on this page and the one used for imports, exports and most bank transfers. The open market rate is the cash rate at exchange companies. It is usually a little higher, and exchange companies quote a buying price (what they pay you) and a selling price (what you pay them). When the gap between the two grows, it is a sign of pressure on the rupee.

What moves the dollar rate in Pakistan

  • Trade gap: Pakistan imports more than it exports, so it needs dollars every month for oil, machinery and food.
  • Remittances: money sent home by overseas Pakistanis is one of the largest sources of dollars and supports the rupee.
  • Reserves and loans: State Bank reserves and inflows from the IMF and other lenders decide how much pressure the rupee can absorb.
  • Interest rates: the State Bank's policy rate and inflation affect how attractive the rupee is to hold.

Why the dollar rate matters for gold, fuel and trading

Gold, oil and most imported goods are priced in dollars, so a weaker rupee makes them more expensive even when world prices do not change. That is why the gold rate in Pakistan and the silver rate move every time the dollar rate moves. Forex traders in Pakistan also fund their accounts in dollars, so the rate changes the rupee value of every deposit and withdrawal.

Frequently asked questions

What is the dollar rate in Pakistan today?

On 14 November 2026 one US dollar is Rs 276.60 in the market (interbank) rate, so 100 dollars is Rs 27,660.

What is the difference between the interbank and open market dollar rate?

The interbank rate is the rate banks trade at with each other and is the rate shown here. The open market rate is what exchange companies charge in cash; it is usually a little higher and has separate buying and selling prices.

Why does the dollar rate change in Pakistan?

The rupee moves with Pakistan's imports and exports, remittances from overseas Pakistanis, foreign exchange reserves, IMF and loan inflows, and the State Bank's interest rate policy.
