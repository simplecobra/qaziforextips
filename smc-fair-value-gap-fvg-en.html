Fair Value Gap (FVG) and IFVG

When price moves very fast in one direction, it leaves behind an empty area where only one side's orders were filled. This is the fair value gap (FVG). In this lesson you will learn to spot FVGs, use their 50% level (CE), understand the inverse FVG (IFVG), and plan a full trade from an FVG.

FVG in simple words

Normally, as price moves up and down, every level gives both buyers and sellers a chance to trade. But when a large order arrives, price leaves so fast that some prices in between only see one-sided trading. This unbalanced area is the FVG (fair value gap), also called an imbalance. The market does not like these gaps. Price often returns later to fill (rebalance) them and then continues in its real direction.

The three-candle rule

An FVG is always made of three candles:

  1. Candle 1: the candle before the move.
  2. Candle 2: the large, strong-bodied move candle (displacement).
  3. Candle 3: the candle after the move.

Bullish FVG: the gap between candle 1's high and candle 3's low, when the two do not touch. Bearish FVG: the gap between candle 1's low and candle 3's high.

RememberAn easy way to find FVGs: look for a big candle, then compare the wick of the candle before it with the wick of the candle after it. If there is empty space between the two wicks, it is an FVG.

CE: the 50% of an FVG

The exact middle of an FVG is called CE (consequent encroachment). It is the most important level of the gap:

  • In a strong FVG, price often reaches the CE and turns.
  • If a body closes beyond the CE, the FVG has weakened.
  • If a body closes beyond the whole FVG, it has failed; it may now become an IFVG.

Judging FVG quality

SignStrong FVGWeak FVG
Middle candleBig body, small wicksSmall body, long wicks
StructureThe move made a BOS or MSSNo high or low broke
LocationWith the bias, in discount or premiumIn the middle of the range
TouchNot filled yetAlready half or fully filled
SizeReasonableTiny (noise) or huge (big stop loss)

FVG and order block together

The best order block is one that left an FVG right after it (lesson 3.1). Together they make a strong POI:

  • The FVG shows the move carried a large order.
  • The order block shows where that order started.
  • Price often fills the FVG first, then touches the order block and turns. So entering in the FVG with the stop behind the order block is a good approach.

IFVG: the inverse FVG

Sometimes price does not just fill an FVG but closes through it with a strong body. That means the large order behind that FVG lost. Now the same zone works the other way:

  • A close above a bearish FVG turns it into support: a bullish IFVG.
  • A close below a bullish FVG turns it into resistance: a bearish IFVG.

An IFVG is especially powerful when a liquidity sweep came before it and the candle that closed through the FVG also made an MSS. That is a clean reversal sign.

Planning an FVG trade

  1. Set the bias and the draw on liquidity.
  2. Find an FVG in the bias direction that formed in the move that made the BOS.
  3. Entry: a limit at the CE (50%), or M1 confirmation inside the FVG.
  4. Stop loss: below the origin of the move (the order block or swing low). A stop just under the FVG is often hit.
  5. Target: the next liquidity. If price closes through the CE, fills the whole FVG and still does not turn, be careful.

Gold example

On Gold M5, price jumped from 4,121 to 4,136 with one large green candle.

  • Candle 1's high is 4,125 and candle 3's low is 4,132. Bullish FVG: 4,125 to 4,132, CE about 4,128.5.
  • The move also broke the 4,130 high, so there is a BOS.
  • Price ran to 4,140, then pulled back to 4,129. Entry with a limit at 4,128.5, stop below the origin of the move at 4,119, risk $9.5.
  • Target: the buy-side liquidity above at 4,148, about $19.5, or 1:2.
  • If any M5 candle had closed with its body below 4,125, the FVG would have failed, and we would now look at the zone as a bearish IFVG.

Real Gold chart example

Gold M15 on 27 Oct 2026 (data: Dukascopy, bid price; Pakistan time):

  • Candle 1 (17:00): high 4,017.6.
  • Candle 2 (17:15): a big green candle, from 4,013.0 to 4,021.0.
  • Candle 3 (17:30): low 4,019.9.
  • The gap between candle 1's high and candle 3's low, 4,017.6 to 4,019.9, is the FVG. Its CE is about 4,018.8.
  • At 17:45 price came back to 4,018.5 (just below the CE), did not close below the FVG, and then ran to 4,064.5 by night.

The FVG was small ($2.3) but formed inside a strong move, which is why it worked.

Common mistakes

Common mistakeTreating every small gap as an FVG. Hundreds form on M1; only those with displacement and a structure break matter.
Common mistakeTrading FVGs against the bias. In a downtrend, bullish FVGs often fill, fail and turn into IFVGs.
Common mistakeNot knowing wick from body. A wick entering the FVG is a fill; a body closing beyond it is a failure.
Common mistakeAssuming every FVG must fill. In a strong trend many FVGs stay unfilled.

Summary

  • An FVG is made of three candles: the gap between the wicks of candle 1 and candle 3.
  • The CE, or 50%, is the most important level.
  • A strong FVG has displacement, a structure break, the bias behind it, and is still unfilled.
  • If a body closes through the whole FVG, it becomes an IFVG and works the other way.

    Questions

    What is an FVG?

    A three-candle pattern where the wicks of candle 1 and candle 3 do not overlap, leaving an empty gap between them.

    What is CE?

    The 50% level of the FVG; in a strong FVG price often turns there.

    What is an IFVG?

    An FVG that price closes through with a body; after that the zone works in the opposite direction.

    Does every FVG get filled?

    No. In a strong trend many FVGs remain unfilled.
