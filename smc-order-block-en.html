Order Block: The Complete Guide

The order block is the most famous point of interest in SMC, and also the most misunderstood. Not every red or green candle is an order block. In this lesson you will learn what a real order block is, how to mark it, how to tell a strong one from a weak one, and how to build the entry, stop loss and target from it.

What is a POI?

POI means Point of Interest: a place on the chart where we expect price to react and where we will look for an entry. In Phase 1 we learned to read bias from structure, and in Phase 2 we learned from liquidity where price is heading. Phase 3 answers the next question: where do we enter? This phase has seven lessons: order block, fair value gap, breaker, mitigation, rejection and propulsion blocks, premium and discount with OTE, and finally the POI checklist. The first and most important is the order block.

Definition

Bullish order block: the last bearish (red) candle right before a strong move up that breaks the previous high (BOS). Bearish order block: the last bullish (green) candle right before a strong move down that breaks the previous low.

Remember the three parts: the last opposite candle, a strong move (displacement), and a break of structure. If any one is missing, the order block is weak, or not an order block at all.

Why order blocks form

A large player cannot fill a whole order at one price. They start buying around the last red candle, and their buying is so large that price shoots up and breaks the previous high. But part of their order is still unfilled, and the part that did fill they do not want to see go into loss. So when price returns to that zone, the same player buys there again. This is called mitigation: the large player protects the earlier position and fills the rest. That is why order blocks so often produce a reaction.

RememberThink of the order block as the large player's shop. They bought stock there, so when price comes back they buy again to protect it.

How to mark an order block

  1. First find the BOS: a strong candle whose body closes above the previous high.
  2. Go to the start of that move and find the last red candle before it began.
  3. Draw the zone from that candle's high to its low (including wicks). Some traders use only the body; see below.
  4. Extend the zone to the right until price touches it again.
  5. Add its 50% level (mean threshold). In a strong order block, price usually does not close below the 50% level.

If two or three red candles sit together before the move and form one block, you can treat the whole group as one zone, but the bigger the zone, the bigger the stop loss.

Full candle or body only?

MethodAdvantageDisadvantage
Full candle (high to low)Fewer missed reactionsBigger stop loss, lower RR
Body only (open to close)Smaller zone, higher RRPrice often turns at the wick and the entry is missed
High to 50%A middle pathNeeds a little practice

Advice for new traders: draw the zone on the full candle, and take the entry from lower timeframe confirmation so the stop loss stays small.

Bearish order block

Exactly the opposite: the last green candle before a strong move down that breaks the previous low. When price returns up into the zone, we look for a sell.

Five signs of a strong order block

  1. BOS: the move after it broke structure. Without a BOS the order block is weak.
  2. Displacement and FVG: the move was fast enough to leave a fair value gap behind. The FVG shows a large order really was there.
  3. Liquidity sweep: before the order block formed, price took a low (SSL) or an inducement.
  4. Unmitigated: price has not yet come back into the zone. The first touch is the strongest.
  5. Right location: a bullish order block sits in discount (below 50% of the range) and agrees with the higher timeframe bias.

The more signs, the stronger the POI. If at least three of the five are not present, skip it.

A weak order block that fails

Here price moved up a little after a red candle, but no high broke and the move had no strength. When price came back, the zone broke straight through. Retail traders buy every zone like this, and their stop losses become the liquidity.

Trading an order block step by step

  1. Bias: H4 and H1 are bullish (Phase 1).
  2. Draw on liquidity: there is clear buy-side liquidity above to act as a target (Phase 2).
  3. POI: mark a strong bullish order block in discount on H1 or M15.
  4. Wait: let price come into the zone. A limit order placed in advance is the aggressive method; confirmation is the conservative one.
  5. Confirmation: a CHoCH on M1 or M5 inside the zone, then enter from the new lower timeframe order block or FVG.
  6. Stop loss: a little below the order block low (or below the confirmation low).
  7. Target: the next liquidity, with the first take profit at the previous high.

Aggressive vs conservative entry

EntryHowAdvantageDisadvantage
AggressiveLimit at the top of the zone or at 50%Never misses the move, good RRFull stop loss if the zone fails
ConservativeWait for an M1 or M5 CHoCH in the zoneAvoids bad zones, smaller stopSometimes price leaves without confirmation

Gold example

Suppose Gold on M15 was falling and a red candle reached 4,100. That last red candle has a high of 4,107 and a low of 4,100. Two large green candles then broke the previous high at 4,112 and closed at 4,116, leaving an FVG from 4,107 to 4,114.

  • Order block: 4,100 to 4,107, with its 50% at about 4,103.5.
  • Price ran to 4,119, then returned and touched 4,105.5.
  • An M1 CHoCH gives an entry at 4,107 with the stop loss at 4,099 (below the order block), a risk of $8.
  • First target: the previous high at 4,119 ($12, 1:1.5); second: the next buy-side liquidity at 4,128 ($21, about 1:2.6).
  • At 0.01 lot, a $1 move in Gold is roughly $1 of profit or loss. If your risk is 2% of a $1,000 account, or $20, then 20 ÷ 8 = 2.5, so 0.02 lot (always round down).

Common mistakes

Common mistakeCalling every last red candle an order block. Without a BOS and displacement it is just a candle.
Common mistakeTrading an order block against the bias. In a downtrend bullish order blocks often fail, because the large player is selling.
Common mistakeBuying a bullish order block in premium. Buying in an expensive area goes against the core SMC rule (see lesson 3.6).
Common mistakeTrusting a zone that has been touched again and again. Each touch uses up the orders sitting there.
Common mistakeStaying in the trade after the zone fails. If a body closes below the order block, the idea is over. Such a zone often becomes a breaker later (lesson 3.3).

Summary

  • A bullish order block is the last red candle before a strong move that breaks structure; a bearish one is the opposite.
  • The large player returns there to protect the position, which is why price reacts.
  • Signs of strength: BOS, FVG, a liquidity sweep, unmitigated, and the right side of discount or premium.
  • Enter on confirmation, stop loss beyond the zone, target the next liquidity.

    Questions

    What is an order block?

    The last opposite candle before a strong move that broke structure; large orders sit there.

    What is the difference between an order block and a supply or demand zone?

    An order block is a specific candle that needs a BOS and displacement after it, while a supply or demand zone is usually the wider base area.

    Which order block is strong?

    One followed by a BOS and an FVG, preceded by a liquidity sweep, still untouched, and sitting in discount or premium in the direction of the bias.

    Which timeframe should I use for order blocks?

    H4, H1 or M15 for the POI, and M5 or M1 for entry confirmation.
